About Chithu Foundation

Practical action in every corner of community life

A non-governmental organization based in Karonga District, Malawi, dedicated to environmental smartness, public cleanliness, and sustainable land stewardship.

Who we are

Chithu Foundation is a non-governmental organization based in Karonga District, Malawi, dedicated to advancing environmental smartness, public cleanliness, and sustainable land stewardship. Founded in response to the growing threat of environmental pollution across the country, the Foundation partners with key community pillars, including schools, hospitals, health facilities, local markets, and farming communities, to transform how society manages waste and protects shared surroundings.

We recognize that clean environments are essential everywhere. Rather than treating environmental protection as an abstract policy issue, Chithu Foundation delivers practical, visible action across every corner of community life.

The people behind the work

A Board that keeps us honest

  • 5 to 9 trustees of integrity, elected at the Annual General Meeting for a three-year term, renewable once.
  • Balanced gender representation on the Board.
  • Meets at least quarterly. Quorum is 50% plus one of all trustees, including the Chair or Vice-Chair.
  • Sets policy, approves budgets and work plans, safeguards assets, appoints independent auditors and oversees the Executive Director.

Open to everyone

  • No discrimination. Membership, volunteering and participation are open regardless of race, sex, religion, political opinion, origin, disability or status.
  • Three kinds of member: Founding Members, Ordinary Members who share our mission, and Institutional Partners such as schools, health facilities and farming clubs.
  • Non-partisan and non-profit: the Foundation does not take political sides.

Every kwacha accounted for

  • Funded by donor grants, philanthropic gifts, CSR partnerships, membership subscriptions and mission-aligned income.
  • Two signatures on every payment: one Board officer (Chair or Treasurer) plus the Executive Director or Finance Officer.
  • Non-distribution rule: income and property serve the mission only; no profits are paid to trustees or members.
  • Audited every year by an independent certified auditor, with statements presented to the AGM and regulators. Financial year: 1 July to 30 June.

Registered and rule-bound

  • A non-profit, non-partisan charitable NGO operating under Malawi's Constitution, the Trustees Incorporation Act and the NGO Act.
  • Headquartered in Karonga District, Northern Region, with a mandate across Karonga and other districts the Board may approve.
  • The constitution can only change by a two-thirds vote of members, after 21 days' written notice.
  • If the Foundation ever closes, remaining assets go to a similar registered non-profit, never to individuals.

Spotlight

Supporting clean & healthy hospital environments

Hospital grounds and waiting areas often struggle with litter, overgrown brush, and uncollected organic waste. We collaborate with healthcare administrators, hospital advisory committees, and local volunteers in Karonga to:

  1. Mobilize organized community clean-up days to clear litter, weeds, and stagnant drainage around hospital and clinic perimeters.
  2. Promote strict waste segregation, keeping general litter and biodegradable waste separate from regulated medical waste managed by health authorities.
  3. Channel safe biodegradable waste (tree trimmings, grass clippings, non-clinical kitchen scraps) toward community composting rather than open burning.

Farmer & Soil Hub

Turning community & institutional waste into agricultural wealth

Fertilizer costs are rising while soils lose organic matter, yet crop residues, market scraps and yard trimmings go unused. We train farmers to make high-grade organic fertilizer right on their own farms.

Stage 1 of 4

Clean-up drives

Schools, hospitals, markets and civic spaces are cleaned, and waste is sorted at the source.

Why waste-to-fertilizer matters

A loop that cleans communities and feeds crops

Cuts input costs

Reduces reliance on expensive imported synthetic fertilizers.

Restores soil structure

Adds humus that holds moisture during dry spells and prevents erosion during heavy rains.

Cleans communities

Diverts organic waste away from roadsides, hospitals, schools, drainage channels, and open dumpsites.

Boosts crop resilience

Feeds beneficial soil microbes that support healthier maize, rice, cassava, and vegetable harvests.

How to make nutrient-rich compost manure

A reliable, low-cost aerobic method (including rapid 21-to-30-day ‘Chimato’ / Bokashi-inspired and layered pit or heap systems) using locally available materials.

Step 1 of 6

Sort & gather your materials

Proper waste sorting is the foundation of environmental smartness. Combine two parts carbon-rich ‘Browns’ with one part nitrogen-rich ‘Greens’.

  • Browns (2 parts): dry maize stalks, rice husks or straw, dry fallen leaves from school and hospital grounds, sawdust, shredded cardboard, dry grass. They feed microbes, prevent foul odors and improve airflow.
  • Greens (1 part): fresh green weeds (before seeding), kitchen vegetable and fruit peels, market produce waste, fresh crop trimmings. They supply nitrogen and heat the pile to kill weed seeds and pathogens.
  • Activators & minerals (a little): cow, goat, pig or poultry manure, wood ash from cooking fires, a shovelful of rich topsoil. They add microbes, balance acidity and add potassium and phosphorus.
Keep out of your compost: plastics, glass, metals, medical waste, and treated wood.

Step 2 of 6

Chop & prepare the site

Select a shaded, well-drained spot near your garden or homestead (or dig a shallow pit 30 cm deep during dry months to conserve moisture). Chop tough stalks like maize stover into smaller pieces so microbes can break them down rapidly.

Pieces of 5–10 cmPit 30 cm deep (dry months)
Step 3 of 6

Build the layers like a lasagna

Start with a 15 cm base layer of coarse twigs or dry stalks for drainage and airflow, then alternate layers. Repeat until the heap reaches 1 to 1.5 meters high.

  • 15 cm base of coarse twigs or dry stalks
  • 15 cm layer of dry ‘Browns’
  • 10 cm layer of fresh ‘Greens’
  • 3–5 cm animal manure and topsoil, lightly sprinkled with wood ash
Step 4 of 6

Moisten & cover

Sprinkle water evenly over each layer as you build. The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge: damp throughout, never waterlogged. Cover the finished heap with banana leaves, dry grass, or a tarpaulin to lock in heat and moisture.

Damp, never waterlogged
Step 5 of 6

Turn & aerate for fast decomposition

Within 3 to 5 days, the center of the pile will heat up, naturally destroying weed seeds and crop diseases. Turn the pile inside-out every 7 to 10 days using a garden fork or hoe, adding water if it feels dry. Regular turning produces finished manure in 4 to 6 weeks.

Heats to 55°C–65°CTurn every 7–10 daysReady in 4–6 weeks
Step 6 of 6

Harvest & apply to your crops

Your organic fertilizer is ready when it turns dark brown-black, crumbles easily in your hand, and smells like fresh forest soil. Apply at planting time, or side-dress growing maize, rice, and vegetables for sustained nutrient release.

1–2 handfuls per planting station

More waste-to-fertilizer techniques we teach

Liquid plant tea (foliar booster)

Soak a porous sack filled with fresh animal manure and nutrient-rich green leaves (such as Tithonia/wild sunflower or moringa leaves) in a drum of water for 7 to 14 days. Dilute 1 part dark tea with 5 parts clean water to create a fast-acting liquid fertilizer for vegetables and seedlings.

Bokashi & kitchen waste fermentation

Households, schools, and institutional kitchens ferment food scraps using inoculated bran and molasses in sealed buckets, turning daily kitchen waste into a nutrient-dense soil conditioner within two weeks without attracting flies or pests.

Crop residue mulching & pit composting

We train farmers never to burn crop residues, a practice that pollutes the air and destroys soil life. Instead, farmers incorporate residues into contour trenches and planting pits to trap rainwater and build long-term soil fertility.

How Chithu Foundation engages local farmers

Community demonstration plots

Hands-on field days in Karonga villages comparing crops grown with waste-derived compost against untreated plots.

Farmer-to-farmer learning clubs

Local grower groups bulk-collect organic market and household waste, share labor for turning compost heaps, and produce organic fertilizer at community scale.

Institution-to-farm linkages

Connecting clean-up drives at schools, markets, and public compounds with local farming clubs so sorted, safe biodegradable waste directly enriches community agriculture.
