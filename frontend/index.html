About us

Practical action in every corner of community life

A non-governmental organisation based in Karonga District, Malawi, working for environmental smartness, public cleanliness and sustainable land stewardship.

Who we are

Chithu Foundation was founded in response to the growing threat of environmental pollution across Malawi. We partner with the pillars of community life (schools, hospitals, health facilities, local markets and farming communities) to change how society manages waste and protects shared surroundings.

Clean environments matter everywhere. Rather than treating environmental protection as an abstract policy issue, we deliver practical, visible action where people learn, heal, trade and farm.

Pollution is one of our country's biggest challenges, and it is time we got smarter with our environment. We are starting with schools in Karonga District, expanding to hospitals and community centres, and equipping farmers to turn organic waste into wealth for their soils.

Our team

Local people, rooted in Karonga District, working with schools, hospitals, markets and farmers to make clean, disciplined environments the everyday norm.

Vision & mission

What we are working towards

A clean, resilient Malawi, built through disciplined stewardship and healthy soils.

Our vision

A clean, resilient and environmentally smart Malawi, where schools, hospitals, public institutions and communities practise disciplined stewardship, and farmers regenerate healthy soils through sustainable waste-to-resource solutions.

Our mission

To champion environmental smartness across Karonga District and beyond by fostering public environmental discipline, leading grassroots clean-up and hygiene initiatives in schools, hospitals and community institutions, and equipping smallholder farmers to turn organic waste into organic fertilizer.

Our core values

Four ideas guide everything we do.

Environmental smartness

Thoughtful, proactive habits that curb pollution, improve sanitation and turn everyday waste into useful community assets.

Civic and environmental discipline

Personal and institutional accountability, so classrooms, hospital wards and markets stay clean, safe and dignified.

Stewardship across institutions

Starting with schools and extending to hospitals, clinics, markets and public offices to build district-wide environmental hygiene.

Circular agriculture

Training farmers to turn household, institutional and crop waste into organic manure, lowering input costs and restoring soil health.

Farmer & Soil Hub

Turning community waste into agricultural wealth

Fertilizer costs are rising while soils lose organic matter, yet crop residues, market scraps and yard trimmings go unused. We train farmers to make high-grade organic fertilizer on their own farms.

Why make fertilizer from waste

One practice cleans our communities and feeds our crops at the same time.

Lower input costs

Less reliance on expensive imported synthetic fertilizers.

Healthier soil structure

Humus holds moisture through dry spells and prevents erosion during heavy rains.

Cleaner communities

Organic waste stays out of roadsides, school and hospital grounds, drains and open dumpsites.

Stronger crops

Compost feeds the soil life that supports healthier maize, rice, cassava and vegetables.

How to make compost manure

A reliable, low-cost method using local materials, including layered heaps, pits and the faster 21-to-30-day Chimato and Bokashi-style systems.

Step 1 of 6

Sort and gather your materials

Good sorting is the foundation of everything. Mix two parts dry, carbon-rich "browns" with one part fresh, nitrogen-rich "greens".

Browns, 2 parts
Dry maize stalks, rice husks or straw, dry fallen leaves, sawdust, shredded cardboard, dry grass. They feed microbes, prevent bad smells and let air through.
Greens, 1 part
Fresh weeds (before they seed), vegetable and fruit peels, market produce waste, fresh crop trimmings. They supply nitrogen and heat the pile, killing weed seeds and disease.
Activators, a little
Cow, goat, pig or poultry manure, wood ash from cooking fires, a shovelful of rich topsoil. They add microbes, balance acidity and add potassium and phosphorus.

Keep out of your compost: plastics, glass, metals, medical waste and treated wood.

Step 2 of 6

Chop and prepare the site

Choose a shaded, well-drained spot near your garden or homestead. In dry months, dig a shallow pit about 30 cm deep to hold moisture. Chop tough stalks such as maize stover into pieces of 5 to 10 cm so microbes can break them down quickly.

Step 3 of 6

Build the layers

Build the heap in layers, like a lasagna, and repeat them until it reaches 1 to 1.5 metres high.

  1. 15 cmBase of coarse twigs or dry stalks, for drainage and air
  2. 15 cmDry browns
  3. 10 cmFresh greens
  4. 3–5 cmAnimal manure and topsoil, lightly sprinkled with wood ash

Step 4 of 6

Moisten and cover

Sprinkle water evenly over each layer as you build. The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge: damp all the way through, never waterlogged. Cover the finished heap with banana leaves, dry grass or a tarpaulin to keep in heat and moisture.

Step 5 of 6

Turn and aerate

Within 3 to 5 days the centre of the pile heats up to 55–65 °C, which destroys weed seeds and crop diseases. Turn the pile inside-out every 7 to 10 days with a fork or hoe, and add water if it feels dry. With regular turning, the manure is ready in 4 to 6 weeks.

Step 6 of 6

Harvest and apply

Your fertilizer is ready when it is dark brown to black, crumbles easily in your hand and smells like fresh forest soil. Apply one to two handfuls per planting station at planting time, or side-dress growing maize, rice and vegetables.

Other techniques we teach

Liquid plant tea

Soak a porous sack of fresh animal manure and green leaves, such as Tithonia (wild sunflower) or moringa, in a drum of water for 7 to 14 days. Mix 1 part of the dark tea with 5 parts clean water for a fast-acting liquid fertilizer for vegetables and seedlings.

Bokashi kitchen-waste fermentation

Households, schools and institutional kitchens ferment food scraps with inoculated bran and molasses in sealed buckets. Within two weeks, kitchen waste becomes a rich soil conditioner, without attracting flies or pests.

Crop residue mulching and pit composting

Burning crop residues pollutes the air and kills soil life. Instead, farmers work residues into contour trenches and planting pits, which trap rainwater and build soil fertility over the long term.

Common questions

What happens to plastic bags and bottles?

They should never be thrown anyhow. At our clean-ups and collection points, plastic bags and bottles are picked up and kept out of drains, fields and public spaces. Plastic bottles are separated from other waste and sent for recycling.

What is a decomposition point?

A place where organic waste (food scraps, leaves, grass and crop residue) is built into layered heaps and left to break down. In four to six weeks it becomes rich organic manure for farms and school gardens.

How can my school work with you?

Schools are our starting point. We run campus clean-up drives, student environmental clubs, waste-sorting bins and school garden compost pits. Use the form above and choose "School".

Do you work with hospitals and clinics?

Yes. We work with healthcare administrators, hospital advisory committees and local volunteers on clean-up days, drainage clearing and compound beautification. General and biodegradable waste is always kept separate from medical waste, which health authorities manage.

Can you hold a composting demonstration in my village?

Yes. We hold hands-on field days in Karonga villages and set up demonstration plots that compare crops grown with compost against untreated plots. Send us a message and choose "Farming club".

How long does compost take to be ready?

The centre of the pile heats up within 3 to 5 days. If you turn it every 7 to 10 days, the manure is ready in 4 to 6 weeks: dark, crumbly and smelling like fresh forest soil.

What should never go into compost?

Plastics, glass, metals, medical waste and treated wood.
